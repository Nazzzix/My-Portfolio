# RydeAssist — Designing Accessible Ride-Booking

Oct 22, 2026 · @Nazor

## Overview

RydeAssist is a ride-booking platform for people who need more than a standard ride: seniors, wheelchair users, visually or hearing impaired riders, and others who may be vulnerable. I designed it end to end as the sole product designer, working directly with the project owner.

## The problem

Standard ride-hailing assumes every rider can find the car, climb in and manage the app unaided. For riders who need assistance, that assumption fails at the first screen.

The product brief positioned RydeAssist as accessibility-first rather than a standard ride-hailing app with accessibility added on. Before designing, I reviewed Uber and Lyft as the baseline riders already know. Three questions shaped the work:

- How can someone with a visual impairment sign up without help?
- How does the platform learn what a rider needs, so the right vehicle and driver are matched without the rider explaining it each time?
- How can family members stay informed without calling or messaging the driver?

## Key design decisions

**1. Voice Assist from the first screen.** The moment the app opens after download, Voice Assist is available, so a rider with a visual impairment can sign up without needing someone else to do it for them. Alongside it, an Accessibility Mode toggle (larger text and voice support) sits at the top of the sign-up and login forms, so accessibility is offered before an account exists, not buried in settings.

**2. Capture needs once, carry them through every ride.** During setup, riders tell the app what they need (for example a ramp, a lift or transfer assistance) in plain, single-purpose cards. Those needs follow the booking and appear on the driver's ride details, so the rider never has to repeat them to each driver.

**3. Family oversight.** Riders may be disabled or vulnerable, so family members can monitor rides and receive updates at each stage, such as booking, pickup and arrival, instead of calling the driver.

**4. A driver flow shaped by local requirements.** The accessibility requirements for drivers in the client's market differed from the ones I was used to, so I redesigned the driver accessibility sign-up flow to fit them. After feedback from the project owner, I also added an in-app space for training guides, so the company can send drivers updated guidance regularly.

**Accessibility standards.** Colour and text were designed to meet WCAG guidelines.

## Handover and reflection

The hardest part of the project was handing the designs to developers I would not be speaking with directly. Because I couldn't explain intent in a conversation, the designs had to carry it themselves.&#32;

Working directly with the project owner meant I carried the product from idea to developer-ready screens. Their feedback shaped the driver sign-up and the in-app training guides, and I carried every change through the rest of the screens to keep them consistent.

**What I took from it.** Accessibility works best as the starting point of the flow, not as a setting. Designing the rider and driver sides together showed me that each decision on one side changes what the other needs to see.
