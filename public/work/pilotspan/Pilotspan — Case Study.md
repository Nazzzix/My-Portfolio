# Pilotspan — Designing a Home Health Management Platform

Oct 22, 2026 · @Nazor

## At a glance

Pilotspan is an all-in-one home health management platform for healthcare agencies. It brings scheduling, payroll, billing, compliance, reporting and communications into one product. As Lead Product Designer, I owned its design direction and designed all 40 of its published capabilities, which the team built, tested and released to production.

## The challenge

Pilotspan was entering an established market. Home health agencies already had software, but two problems kept coming up in the products I reviewed.

- **Complexity.** Interfaces and workflows were complicated, so everyday clinical and administrative tasks were hard to find and carry out.
- **Outdated interfaces.** Many products looked and behaved as if they had not been redesigned in years, which hurt usability and how information was presented.

A home health agency runs on many connected tasks: scheduling, time tracking and payroll, clinical documentation, claims, compliance and reporting. The design opportunity was a modern, coherent platform that simplifies those operations in one place.

Pilotspan also had to work as a business. It needed a recognisable brand, and a subscription model that customers could understand.

## Research

My research was a competitive analysis. I reviewed existing home health management platforms, without naming them here, and looked at:

- how they organised their features
- where their interfaces were hard to use or unnecessarily complex
- where a more intuitive, modern experience could do better

It informed the direction I took: a simpler structure, one consistent design language, and clear presentation of dense information.

## My role and ownership

I owned the design direction for both the brand and the product, and I designed every feature the product publishes: 8 main features and 32 sub-features, 40 capabilities in all. They cover scheduling and workforce management, time tracking and payroll, billing and claims, quality assurance and compliance, reporting and analytics, communications, and AI-assisted features.

- **Brand and visual direction:** I created the brand identity and visual language for the business and the software.
- **Product structure:** I defined the information architecture, navigation and page layouts.
- **Interface and design system:** I designed the screens and built the reusable components and patterns behind them.
- **Business decisions:** I took part in product decisions, business alignment, pricing and subscriptions, working with the wider team.
- **Coordination:** As I came to understand the product, I helped clarify requirements and keep design and delivery aligned. This was informal and sat alongside my design role.

I did not own every business or engineering decision, and I did not build the product.

## Key design decisions

### 1. One navigation shell for the whole product

With this many capabilities, the risk was a collection of separate tools. Every screen sits inside one frame instead: a persistent top navigation (Dashboard, Communications, Calendar, People, Manager, Billing, Reports, Settings) and a page title bar with a back control. Staff move between clinical, scheduling and billing work without relearning where things are.

### 2. A softer, less clinical interface

Many existing platforms feel clinical, which can leave clinicians feeling overwhelmed. I moved the interface towards something more modern, using softer, rounded content boxes and buttons to take the edge off the platform. I also added metric count cards to all features, so clinicians see the numbers that matter at a glance, such as the number of active patients, the number of due recertifications and the number of NOAs.

### 3. One brand from website to product

I carried the same logo, colours and typography from the marketing website through sign-in, verification and password reset, and into the product, so the brand and the software read as one.

## From design to production

The designs went all the way through delivery:

1. **Designed:** 8 main features and 32 sub-features.
2. **Built:** implemented by the development team.
3. **Tested:** internal testing and user testing.
4. **Released:** all 40 capabilities were pushed to production during my involvement.

These are delivery outcomes. I have no measured results for efficiency, satisfaction or revenue, so I do not claim any.

I designed with the guidance of the product consultant. The developers built each feature and pushed it for implementation testing before it moved to production, where real users could test it.

## Working with the team

I worked with product managers and engineers throughout. I explained the reasoning behind interface decisions and helped clarify requirements as questions came up. The component library and shared patterns gave the team a common base to build from.

Every design went through a set process before it shipped:

1. The product consultant, a specialist in the field, reviewed the design to make sure it met the standard and followed compliance requirements.
2. The front-end and back-end developers were walked through the requirements and acceptance criteria before the build.
3. Once built and deployed to staging, I tested for UI accuracy and function, and the consultant tested that it met every requirement.
4. It then moved to production, where users could test it and give feedback.

## What I took from it

- Product design also needs business understanding. Plans, pricing and billing shaped the interface as much as the workflows did.
- Leading the design means setting a direction that holds across many screens, not only polishing individual ones.
- A shared component library helps the whole team, not only the look of the product.
- Working beyond the interface, by clarifying requirements and connecting people, helped the work move towards release.
